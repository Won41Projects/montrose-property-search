# Paramedicine and where the ambulance goes

A short note for Dr. Prince. Two questions from the discussion: what community paramedicine has been shown to do, and whether ambulance patients commonly have to pass a rural hospital for a larger one.

## Community paramedicine

Community paramedicine, also called mobile integrated health, sends a paramedic to selected patients for follow-up, frequent-user care, or a low-acuity 911 call that may not need an emergency department. It is not an emergency department, and it cannot admit, image, or keep someone overnight.

The utilization evidence is real and narrow.

- A 2024 systematic review of 11 studies found that, for the patients a community paramedic attended, emergency-department transport was 14 to 78 percent lower than usual ambulance care. The higher-quality studies were about 50 to 54 percent lower. Costs fell in the studies that measured them, because an emergency visit was avoided, while the ambulance service itself spent more time and training. The authors’ condition was enough non-emergency demand to cover that cost. These are patients already selected for the program, not every 911 call in a county. (Wilkinson-Stokes and colleagues, *Applied Health Economics and Health Policy*, 2024.)
- An exploratory meta-analysis of 12 studies found programs associated with fewer later emergency visits, about a 44 percent lower risk (risk ratio 0.56, confidence interval 0.42 to 0.74). Every study was observational, and the results disagreed with each other (I-squared over 90 percent). (Lurie and colleagues, *American Journal of Emergency Medicine*, 2023.)

What is not shown: a mortality benefit, or a substitute for a night emergency department. Medicare tested a related idea, paying ambulances for an alternate destination or treatment in place. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services ended that model on December 31, 2023, two years early, because participation and the number of interventions were lower than expected. Treatment in place under that model also required a clinician other than the paramedic. (CMS, Emergency Triage, Treat, and Transport Model.)

Paramedicine can take some non-emergency calls off the emergency department. It does not replace the department, a bed, or imaging.

## Do most ambulance patients have to go somewhere else?

No. A rural hospital is the destination for the large majority of emergency visits. Transfer is common only inside the smaller group that needs a service the rural hospital does not have.

The figure that sounds like “many” uses a different denominator. Among Medicare patients who had emergency care at a critical access hospital and an inpatient stay the same day, the inpatient stay was at a different hospital 28.4 percent of the time. (Casey, McCullough, and Kreiger, University of Minnesota Rural Health Research Center, 2014.) That is the share of admitted patients who needed a larger hospital, not the share of ambulance rides.

A few percent need to go on: major trauma, a heart attack that needs a cath lab, a stroke that needs a procedure the local hospital cannot do. Those patients are stabilized and transferred, or flown. They are not most rides. Weakness, injury, abdominal pain, breathing trouble, and chest pain are the common ambulance problems, and a critical access emergency department is built to sort them.

## What that means on the West End

The filed service plan reports the 2025 counts. EMS transported 132 patients to a hospital. Thirty-two of those were by helicopter. EMS had another 141 calls that did not end in a hospital transport. The drive from the clinic to Montrose Regional Health is at least 1.75 hours and 86.5 miles.

All 132 hospital transports leave today because there is no hospital here. That is not evidence they would still have to leave if there were one. The 32 helicopter flights are the local group already judged to need speed or a higher level of care. Some of those would still go to Montrose or Grand Junction. The ground transports are the rides a local emergency department is meant to receive.

One caution from the Medicare study belongs in the same paragraph. Rural emergency departments matched urban 30-day mortality for sepsis, stroke, and heart attack, and they transferred more of those patients. Symptom complaints, including chest pain, nausea, and abdominal pain, had higher mortality after a rural visit. A local emergency department has to be good at those complaints, and willing to transfer early, or the building does not fix the outcome.
