# Response to Community Questions on the Proposed Critical Access Hospital

> Status: DRAFT talking points / letter basis. Tone: respectful — these are
> exactly the questions a community should ask before voting. Every figure
> traces to the filed WEHSD service plan, the district's published materials,
> federal data, or the calculator at montrose-property-search.onrender.com.

---

## 1. "The payer mix here (75% Medicare/Medicaid, 15% commercial) makes revenues unreliable."

You're right about the mix — and that mix is precisely why a **Critical Access
Hospital** is the right structure, not a risk to it.

- CAHs are not paid like ordinary hospitals. **Medicare reimburses CAHs at 101%
  of allowable costs** for inpatient, outpatient, and swing-bed services. For a
  regular hospital, a 75% government payer mix is a financial death sentence;
  for a CAH, it is the *revenue floor*. The heavier our Medicare mix, the more
  predictable — not less — the largest revenue stream becomes. The CAH program
  exists because Congress recognized that communities exactly like ours can't
  survive on commercial-insurance economics.
- On commercial patients "avoiding" a CAH: the services that drive a small
  hospital's commercial revenue — the emergency room, imaging, labs — are
  chosen by **proximity, not brand**. Nobody with chest pain or a broken arm
  drives 100 extra miles to make a statement about payer demographics. Rangely,
  Meeker, and Holyoke do not lose their commercially insured ER patients to
  Grand Junction.
- The alternative keeps the *worst* of both worlds: the clinic today is paid
  clinic rates with the same payer mix, minus the cost-based protection —
  which is why the filed service plan states it would run a **$500,000 annual
  deficit** without the Public Safety Sales Tax and year-to-year grants.

## 2. "Five years is too long; why pay $1M/year while we wait? Are consultants stockpiling cash?"

We share the urgency — and would welcome pressure to move faster. But the
timeline and the cash build-up are driven by lenders and federal regulators,
not by consultants:

- The model finances roughly **82% of the project with debt** (USDA/rural
  facility lending). Those lenders require environmental review, completed
  design, and — critically — **demonstrated equity and cash reserves** before
  they will close. The "$3.5 million in cash flow over five years" isn't money
  sitting idle; it is the down payment and days-cash-on-hand that make an
  $82 million loan closeable. Accumulating those reserves is the *fastest*
  path to opening, not a delay tactic.
- CMS will not certify a CAH until the building is constructed, staffed, and
  surveyed. Design-financing-construction on projects this size runs 4–6 years
  nationally; Rangely's facility followed a similar arc.
- Taxpayers are not waiting five years for *all* benefit. Levy funds stabilize
  and expand the clinic immediately (ending the annual grant scramble),
  extend hours toward after-hours care — the #1 need in the community survey
  (73.6% of respondents) — and everything is governed by a **publicly elected
  board** filing public budgets under state Local Government Budget Law, with
  annual audits. If anyone believes a dollar is being parked improperly, the
  board meetings and budgets are open. Better yet: run for the board.
- Where we agree completely: use proven designs. The board toured Rangely for
  exactly that reason, and pushing to compress design time with an existing
  CAH template is a fair demand the community should keep making.

## 3. "The $375K county payment, $600K 340B, and $2.5M provider fee could all go away."

Stress-testing revenue is the right instinct. Three responses:

- **None of those is the engine.** The engine is cost-based Medicare
  reimbursement plus the mill levy — the two most durable revenue sources
  available to any rural facility. The pro forma dropping county funding in
  2033 is the *conservative* assumption you'd want: it plans for
  self-sufficiency rather than counting on it forever.
- Risk cuts both ways, and the **status quo is the riskier portfolio**. The
  clinic today survives on annually renewed grants and a county sales tax —
  the filed plan says plainly it would otherwise run $500K/year in the red.
  A taxing district with cost-based reimbursement is the *hedge against*
  funding turbulence, not exposure to it.
- On federal changes: the new **$50 billion Rural Health Transformation Fund
  exists precisely because Congress knows rural hospitals need support** —
  and those dollars will flow to rural *hospitals and districts*, not to
  communities that chose to remain clinic-only. Recent federal proposals have
  consistently carved out and protected rural facilities. Being an organized
  district with a facility in progress positions the West End to receive that
  money; standing pat guarantees ineligibility for most of it.

## 4. "241 EMS patients a year is fewer than one a day — $1M a year for that?"

The 241 figure counts only **EMS-involved** patients — the sickest tip of the
iceberg. Most emergency room patients arrive by private vehicle, not
ambulance:

- At the national rate of 47 ED visits per 100 people (CDC/NCHS — rural rates
  run higher), our 2,259 residents generate roughly **1,000–1,100 emergency
  visits a year**, about three a day — before counting travelers on the
  highways, and before counting the visits that simply don't happen today
  because Montrose is an hour-plus away. Deferred care is invisible in EMS
  logs; it shows up later as advanced disease.
- The feasibility model projects ~2,500 patients in year one, growing toward
  7,500 annual visits at a well-run facility — consistent with utilization
  data, not with the EMS-transfer count.
- **Swing beds are not a revenue afterthought** — Medicare pays CAH swing beds
  on the same cost basis as everything else, and they are among the most
  stabilizing service lines small hospitals run. The 12–14 long-term-care
  beds mean our elders rehab and age *here* instead of being placed
  out-of-county, which the service plan documents is happening now.
- On surgery: agreed, and the plan agrees — it does not bank on a resident
  surgeon. Visiting proceduralists (colonoscopies, minor procedures) are the
  standard CAH model. Note that our economic case *deliberately excludes*
  surgical revenue.
- Finally, the per-patient math is the wrong frame for emergency capacity —
  we don't divide the fire department's budget by the number of structure
  fires. But even on pure dollars: about **$25,000 a day (~$9 million a
  year) currently leaves this community** in healthcare spending and medical
  travel costs, against a levy of roughly $950,000. Recapturing even a
  fraction of the outflow dwarfs the levy — see the live counter and full
  breakdown at montrose-property-search.onrender.com.

## 5. "Housing and staffing will make this impossible or agency-cost-prohibitive."

This is the most serious operational challenge, and it deserves a serious
answer rather than a dismissal:

- Note that this concern and #2 answer each other: the phased five-year
  timeline the critique wants to halve is also what gives hiring and housing
  time to develop. Roughly a third of positions are on the long-term-care
  side — jobs that can be **grown locally**: Skillful-West End is already
  putting local high schoolers through CNA licensure, and Nucla High has an
  active health-careers pipeline. Hospitals this size hire from home first.
- 70–80 stable jobs averaging well above area wages is itself the anchor that
  makes housing investment financeable — the West End Vision Project pairs
  the two deliberately. The district can also pursue employer-assisted
  housing, as peer CAHs have.
- Rangely — a town our size, arguably more remote — staffs its CAH today.
  So do Meeker, Holyoke, and Kremmling. Hard is not the same as impossible,
  and cost-based reimbursement recognizes actual (including premium) labor
  costs in the Medicare rate, which softens agency exposure relative to a
  regular hospital.

## 6. "How many CAHs were built in Colorado recently, and are they profitable?"

Great question — and we'd encourage everyone to look, because the record is
reassuring:

- Colorado has over 30 operating CAHs. Several communities our size have
  built **new replacement facilities** in recent years — Rangely, Meeker
  (Pioneers Medical Center), Holyoke (Melissa Memorial), Kremmling (Middle
  Park Health) — and continue operating. Their Form 990s are public;
  we welcome that analysis and will help anyone pull them.
- And there is now a designed **fallback**: since 2023, federal law offers the
  Rural Emergency Hospital designation — 24/7 emergency plus outpatient
  services with a federal facility payment (~$3.4M/year) — created
  specifically so facilities like ours have a sustainable model even if full
  CAH volumes disappoint. A district has options; a clinic has none of them.

## On the suggested alternatives

- **"Work with Norwood":** Uncompahgre Medical Center is a clinic, like Basin —
  no emergency room, no beds, no imaging beyond basics, 30+ mountain miles
  away. Two clinics cooperating still leaves the West End with zero 24/7
  emergency care, zero swing beds, zero long-term care, and the same
  $25,000/day flowing out. Collaboration is welcome — it is not a substitute.
- **"Just tax to upgrade the clinic":** an upgraded clinic is still paid clinic
  rates with the same payer mix and still closes at 5 p.m. It cannot address
  the community's #1 surveyed need (after-hours emergency care), cannot keep
  our elders in-county, and captures almost none of the outflow. It would be
  a permanent subsidy with no path to self-sufficiency — the exact cycle the
  filed plan documents the clinic has been trapped in since 2013.

## The bottom line

Every year without a hospital, roughly **$9 million leaves this community**,
our neighbors drive an hour-plus for basic care, and our elders are placed in
facilities out of county. The levy asks about $950,000 a year to reverse
that — with cost-based federal reimbursement doing the heavy lifting, an
elected board and public audits watching every dollar, proven peer facilities
across rural Colorado, and a federal fallback model if projections fall
short. The riskiest option on the table is the status quo.
