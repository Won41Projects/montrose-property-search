## Settled answers

**Clinic fee versus hospital payment.** UMC is paid a flat fee per office visit. In 2026 Medicare’s FQHC base is about $208, and Medicare pays 80 percent. UMC also gets a federal clinic grant. The fee does not grow because someone came in at 2 a.m. or needed a bed. UMC cannot bill a hospital stay. A critical access hospital is paid about 101 percent of allowable cost for the emergency visit, the overnight stay, and the swing bed. A 2 percent federal sequestration cut brings the check closer to 99 percent, and not every expense counts. That payment is what makes staying open all night possible.

**$9 million versus a $19 million hospital budget.** The $9 million is the estimate of care and travel already leaving: about $8.1 million of care for 2,259 residents plus about $1.2 million of travel, roughly $9.3 million. Per resident per year, the bands used were pharmacy $1,200–$1,400, emergency and urgent care $620–$800, primary care $520–$680, imaging $200–$280, lab $200–$280, inpatient and observation $280–$360, and therapy $120–$240. Medicare and insurance already pay most of that $9 million, just to other hospitals. The $19 million is a later operating budget for a larger service area, a long-term care unit, inpatient days, and diagnostics, not a claim that the same care suddenly costs double. The 2033 revenue stack in the projection was about $22.5 million net patient collections, $2.5 million Colorado provider fee, $600,000 340B, $597,000 grants, and $1.07 million levy. Established Colorado critical access hospitals more often see about $1.3–$1.8 million net from the provider fee, not $2.5 million.

**Rangely and a smaller first phase.** Rangely District Hospital, CCN 061307, FY2024: about $19.4 million net patient revenue, $23.2 million operating expenses, a $3.2 million operating loss, covered by tax and other non-operating revenue on roughly $280 million of assessed value from oil and gas. The West End assessed value in the plan is about $47.5 million, so 20 mills raises about $950,000 to $1.07 million. Statutory general-obligation debt is capped at 20 percent of assessed value, about $9.5 million. The $82.2 million, 65,000-square-foot concept does not fit that tax base. The working conclusion Troy accepted: phase it. A first phase on the order of $50–65 million all-in, with $25–35 million of USDA Community Facilities debt. USDA direct loans are 40-year fixed; an intermediate rate cited in the work was about 4.625 percent. Precedents mentioned: Sublette County, Wyoming; Sullivan County, Missouri; Heart of America, North Dakota. Doing nothing keeps the clinic on annual grants and a county sales tax, and the filed plan says the clinic would run about a $500,000 deficit without those.

**Election mechanics.** Question 6A organizes the district. Question 6B authorizes the mill levy and keeping the revenue. It does not authorize bonds. Multi-year debt still needs a separate voter approval under TABOR. “Certified by December 15” is C.R.S. 39-5-128: the district must certify the mill levy to the county by December 15 or that levy misses the next tax year. Four outcomes: both pass; only 6A; only 6B; neither. Only 6A creates a district with no assured levy. Only 6B does not create the district, so the levy has nothing to attach to. Neither leaves today’s clinic.

## Note for Dr. Prince

Paramedicine helps a selected group. It does not replace an emergency department.

Wilkinson-Stokes and colleagues, *Applied Health Economics and Health Policy*, 2024: among patients a community paramedic attended, emergency transport was 14 to 78 percent lower than usual ambulance care. Higher-quality studies were about 50 to 54 percent lower. That is not half of all 911 calls. Lurie and colleagues, *American Journal of Emergency Medicine*, 2023: later emergency visits about 44 percent lower (risk ratio 0.56, interval 0.42 to 0.74), but every study was observational and they disagreed sharply. No mortality benefit is shown in those reviews. Medicare’s Emergency Triage, Treat, and Transport model ended December 31, 2023, two years early, for low participation. Treatment in place required a clinician other than the paramedic.

Transfers are a few percent of emergency visits, not most ambulance rides.

- Rural Medicare emergency visits transferred, 2011–2015: 6.2 percent, versus 2.0 percent urban. Critical access hospitals showed the same pattern. Thirty-day mortality for sepsis, stroke, and heart attack was similar to urban emergency departments. Symptom complaints, including chest pain, nausea, and abdominal pain, had higher mortality after a rural visit. Greenwood-Ericksen and colleagues, *JAMA Network Open*, 2021.
- Injured rural ambulance patients later transferred, Oregon and Washington, 2011: 3.2 percent, versus 2.7 percent urban. Most high-risk rural trauma patients went to the nearest hospital first. Newgard and colleagues, *JAMA Surgery*, 2017.
- All U.S. emergency visits transferred, 2018: about 2.8 percent. Small emergency departments about 5 percent. Emergency Department Benchmarking Alliance, via *ACEP Now*.
- Colorado trauma-registry patients taken from the scene by helicopter, 2009–2013: 2.7 percent. Another 3.0 percent were flown between hospitals. The registry misses many patients who stay at a level IV or V hospital. Jansen and colleagues, *Journal of Trauma*, 2018.

The “many” figure is a different denominator. Of Medicare patients who had critical-access emergency care and an inpatient stay the same day, the stay was at another hospital 28.4 percent of the time. Casey, McCullough, and Kreiger, University of Minnesota Rural Health Research Center, 2014. That is admitted patients, not every ride.

West End service plan, 2025: EMS transported 132 patients to a hospital, 32 by helicopter, plus 141 calls with no hospital transport. Montrose is at least 1.75 hours and 86.5 miles. All 132 leave today because there is no hospital here. The helicopter flights are the group already judged to need speed or a higher level. The ground transports are the rides a local emergency department is for.
